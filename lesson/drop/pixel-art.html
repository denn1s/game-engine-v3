The Value of One Pixel

A drawing class for people who don't draw. The goal today is not skill — it's losing the fear of the empty canvas, and learning that at 8×8 you don't draw the thing, you draw the idea that identifies the thing.

Before class

Send this the day before

Aseprite is paid (~$20, free trial available). Nobody should spend class time on a purchase decision, so give them the options up front:

  • Aseprite trial — full interface, can't save. Fine for today.
  • LibreSprite — free fork of Aseprite, near-identical interface. Everything you demo transfers.
  • Browser fallback — Pixilart, Piskel, or Lospec's Pixel Editor. Zero install, works for an 8×8.

Say explicitly: if the install fights you, open the browser one and don't miss the class over it. Anyone still stuck at 0:45 uses the browser tool and loses nothing.

The shape of the class

poll → resolution → the cat (demo) → silhouette → the word → draw → gallery

The demo subject is easy and canonical; the exercise subject is hard and has no canonical form. That contrast is the whole design of the class — an 8×8 cat has a known solution they can copy, an 8×8 lobster does not, so they have to decide what a claw is worth in pixels. Don't skip the cat, and don't make the exercise subject easier.

Run sheet

0:005 min

The poll — which one first

Live poll in the first five minutes, not sent out beforehand. An async poll comes back with four responses and now you've manufactured evidence that nobody cares; live, everyone present votes and turnout is 100% by construction.

"Two classes, art and narrative. You're both getting both. This vote decides which one is today."

Framing matters: ordering, never preference. A 4–3 vote on "which do you want" leaves three people who lost. A 4–3 vote on "which first" leaves nobody out.

0:0510 min

Resolution is a code decision, not an art decision

Open conf.lua and the viewport code from 04.5. Teaching point: they already wrote the integer scaler. The reason it's integer-only is the thing they're about to draw.

Native canvas 640×400 (the PC-98 resolution Princess Maker and Tokimeki were drawn for), window at ×2 = 1280×800, letterbox the remainder. Then show the failure: scale a sprite by ×1.5 and let them see that some source pixels become two screen pixels and their neighbours become one. The grid stops being a grid.

"Every pixel you place today gets multiplied by two, exactly, forever. That's why you get so few of them — and why they're worth something."

0:1515 min

Aseprite, and the cat

Tour the interface while drawing, not before. Don't teach tools — teach that the canvas is not scary. Six things, that's all:

  • New file, 8×8 — and let them react to how small that dialog number looks.
  • Zoom — the canvas is huge on screen and tiny in reality. Name that gap now.
  • Preview window — the single most important panel. Real size, always open, always the truth.
  • Pencil, eraser, bucket, eyedropper. Four tools. There is no fifth tool today.
  • The palette — pick four swatches and say out loud that the rest of the palette is off-limits.
  • The timeline — point at it, say "this is how sprites animate, and we are not touching it today." Naming it stops it from being a mystery.

Then draw the cat, badly, out loud. Erase things. Undo. Start the ear over twice. Say that you're doing it. This is the actual curriculum of the beat — they need to watch someone competent flail, or they'll think their own flailing means they can't draw.

The money moment — three single-pixel edits, once the cat reads:

  • Delete one ear pixel → it's a bear, or nothing at all.
  • Move one eye pixel down → it's now sleepy, or sad, or dead.
  • Add one pixel at the base → it was standing, now it's sitting.

"At this size there's no such thing as a small change. You have 64 decisions total, and you just watched three of them change the species, the mood, and the pose."

0:3010 min

Silhouette first, and the four-color budget

Teaching point: pixel art is subtractive. You are not shrinking a drawing — you're deciding which 64 squares matter. Beginners add interior detail to a shape that doesn't read yet, and interior detail cannot rescue a bad silhouette.

Rules for today, four of them:

  • Silhouette before anything. Block the whole shape in one flat color and check the preview window. If you can't tell what it is in flat black, adding colors won't help.
  • Four colors means three inks. One of your four is the background. Spend the other three as darkest, lightest, one middle — value contrast is what makes a shape read, not hue.
  • No anti-aliasing. A softened edge at 8×8 is a wasted pixel, and one wasted pixel out of 64 is 1.5% of your entire drawing.
  • Check at 1:1 constantly. Everything looks good at 800% zoom. The preview window is the only honest judge.

0:405 min

The word

Keep it secret until now. Everyone draws the same subject — that's non-negotiable, because the gallery only teaches anything if the drawings are comparable. Fifteen different lobsters is a lesson; fifteen different animals is a slideshow.

Lobster

Why it works: one iconic feature (the claws) that cannot fit. They will spend two pixels on a claw and discover that two pixels can mean "claw" if the rest of the shape agrees. That discovery is the class.

Alternates, same property — an unmistakable feature with no room for it: octopus, hedgehog, lighthouse, pineapple, umbrella. Avoid anything with a known 8×8 solution they can recall from a game.

The brief, said once and pasted in chat: 8×8 canvas, 4 colors including background, 25 minutes, subject is the lobster. Ugly is expected. Unfinished is fine. Empty is the only failure.

0:4525 min

Draw — and you draw too

This is the answer to "what do I do while they work." You draw the lobster as well, on stream, in silence, and you do not show it until the gallery. It solves four problems at once: no dead air, no one being watched, you model the flailing, and if nobody shares you still have something to reveal.

What not to do: don't ask "how's it going?" every three minutes. In a remote room that reads as surveillance and it makes people hide unfinished work — which is exactly the behavior you're trying to kill.

  • Timer on screen, visible the whole time. Silence is only comfortable when people can see how much of it is left.
  • Warnings at 10 min and 3 min left. Nothing else spoken.
  • At the 10-minute mark, show your own canvas mid-mess — half-erased, wrong, unfinished. One sentence: "this is what mine looks like right now."
  • Chat stays open for stuck people. Answer in chat, not out loud.
1:1012 min

The gallery — 3, 2, 1, paste

This is the answer to "what if nobody shares." The reason nobody shares in a remote class is that somebody has to go first. Remove that:

  • Screenshot pasted into chat. Not a file, not a link, not a folder upload. Lowest possible friction.
  • Count down out loud — 3, 2, 1 — and everyone pastes at once, including you. Simultaneity is the whole trick.
  • Then look at them together. Point at the same decision across drawings: "how many pixels did each of you spend on the claw?" Comparison, never ranking.

Fallback, if it's still crickets: have three of your own 8×8 lobsters ready — one too literal, one that reads cleanly, one that cheats cleverly — and critique your own work out loud for the full twelve minutes. Nobody gets put on the spot, and the lesson still lands. Prepare these before class; they cost you ten minutes and they're insurance you'll reuse every semester.

The one question to ask about every drawing: can you tell what it is in the preview window, at real size, without being told? That's the only criterion today.

Cut line

If installs eat the first twenty minutes, cut the silhouette beat (0:30) and go straight from the cat to the word. The drawing and the gallery are the class; the four rules can be pasted into chat as text and read at home. Never protect the theory at the cost of the practice.

1:228 min

Homework, and the three-minute ramp

Assign the homework, then spend three minutes only on value ramps — just enough that they aren't lost. Do not teach shading properly today. You'll teach it next class from their own 16×16s, where it means something because they've already tried and hit the wall.

The three-minute version: pick a base color; the shadow is darker and shifted toward blue/purple, the highlight is lighter and shifted toward yellow. Never just lower the brightness of the same hue — that's the difference between "muddy" and "alive," and it's the whole reason we blend in HSL rather than RGB.

"Next class we open with all of your 16×16s on one screen, and we learn shading by looking at what you already did."

Homework

  • The same lobster at 16×16, in 8 colors. Same subject on purpose — the comparison between your two versions is the assignment. Four times the pixels: now you can afford an outline, a shadow side, and a highlight.
  • A palette for your own game — 8 to 16 colors, exported as a swatch. Not a mood board: actual colors you commit to. Steal shamelessly from Lospec's palette list. One sentence on why these colors and not others.
  • One asset from your own game, at the real size it will ship at. Pick from the budget table above — a 32px cursor is a perfectly honest choice.
  • The phrase pools — see the card below. This one is due regardless of which lesson we did today.

The phrase pools — feeds next class

Assign this today, whichever lesson won the poll

Next class builds the pack generator, and it needs a phrase pool to draw from. If this homework doesn't go out today, that lesson slips a week. It's ~30 minutes of writing and it needs no theory.

10–15 fragments per stat, plain text, three headed lists — you'll paste them straight into a Lua table.

statregisterexample
Intassertions, facts, corrections"Actually, the funny thing is…"
Chawarmth, affirmation, humor"Haha, no way, tell me more—"
Senobservations, left turns"Doesn't this remind you of…"

The one hard constraint — and it's a real engineering lesson: each fragment must work as a reply to anything. The generator pairs a random phrase with a random topic, so a line that only makes sense after one specific subject will break the moment it's dealt. Write openings, not answers. That constraint is what makes text data-driven instead of scripted.

Notes for a small remote elective

  • The gallery has to work with four people. It does — four lobsters side by side is still a comparison. Don't apologize for the turnout or count heads out loud; it reframes a working class as a failed one.
  • Absentees are by design here. Share this run sheet, the word, and the brief in the class channel so someone who missed it can do the exercise alone and paste into the gallery late. Their 8×8 in Thursday's chat is worth more than perfect attendance.
  • Everything today is engine-adjacent on purpose. Resolution, integer scaling, the sprite budget, HSL vs RGB — these are the same decisions the card renderer will make in code. This is not a break from the course.
